## 5. Backtest (90 days of synthetic purchase history, demo policy)

35 purchases replayed: **16 auto-approved, 16 escalated, 3 blocked** by the circuit breaker.

- Realized savings (auto-approved only): **$1,198.53** (6.61% of what humans paid for those).
- Potential savings if the manager approves escalations: $2,437.46.
- Of the realized savings, $1,138.76 comes from picking the cheaper vendor; negotiation adds $59.77.
- Seat pruning would flag 201 of 848 seats, about $4,693.50 a month of $16,902.25.

Assumptions:
- Replays demand (sku, quantity, date, benchmark), not the human's vendor or price choices.
- The agent buys from the cheapest approved vendor after up to max_rounds of counters at the benchmark price.
- Vendor price lists and concession behaviour come from our simulated vendors, so savings depend on that configuration.
- Escalated purchases are assumed approved by the manager and count toward the monthly cap.
- Blocked purchases (circuit breaker) are not executed and never counted as savings.
- Non-USD purchases are excluded from savings (no FX data).
- Duplicate orders are not detected by any verifier rule, so a duplicate would be replayed as a second purchase.

## 6. Limitations (read before quoting any number)

- The company, its SaaS seats, the asset register, usage logs and the vendors are simulated. Benchmark prices come from the real Channel3 catalog.
- The generator makes cleanly separable behaviour, so ML metrics are upper bounds and measure learning of the generator, not of real offices.
- Planted anomalies are 30% of the history; real fraud is far rarer, which flatters precision.
- Savings depend on our simulated vendors' prices and concessions.
- The verifier, not any model, decides whether money moves. Every ML score is advisory.
- No rule detects duplicate orders; the ML anomaly service flags them but the verifier does not.
